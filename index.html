Research audit protocols MAD v2.1 Source on GitHub

Four models read your paper in four different roles, then cross-examine each other

Round 1 gives you four independent readings, each blind to the others. Round 2 shows every model what the other three said and asks it to kill the weak points. A fresh conversation then judges what survived, and you check the survivors against your own file before you believe any of it.

Built for work where missing a serious flaw is expensive: a journal revision, a major grant, a job-market paper, a referee report. It is not worth running on everything.

By Zuzana Iršová and Tomáš Havránek, Charles University · meta-analysis.cz

The page runs entirely in your browser. Your document is never uploaded here, and nothing you type on this page leaves it.

Before you paste anything

Whatever you upload goes to four companies rather than one. That is the price of the coverage. Turn off training on your data where the provider allows it (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) and check what your institution allows before you start. Opting out of training does not delete what you already sent.

If the document is not yours, the decision is not yours either. A manuscript you are refereeing, a grant you are evaluating, a colleague’s unpublished draft: uploading it means making a confidentiality choice on someone else’s behalf, four times over. Many journals and funders forbid it outright, and some rule out AI assessment of a proposal altogether, including a model you run on your own machine. Find the policy that binds you before you start, not after.

The run

MAD v2.1 · four models

  1. Set the lineup

    Four models, four different jobs. Which seat goes to which model is our choice rather than a finding: we give the two heaviest analytical seats to the models we have found steadiest on long technical documents, and the devil’s advocate seat to the one whose answers tend to diverge most from the rest, because that seat exists to break a consensus rather than to be right.

    A vague label such as “tough referee” gets you four copies of one review. What produces different failure modes is a concrete prior, so each role below says what it suspects before it has read anything. Edit them freely if your document calls for something else. You can also run different models in these seats, though the page keeps these four names in the prompts and the bundle template, so change the names in your own bundle headings to match.

  2. Round 1: four independent readings

    Upload: the document, to each of four fresh conversations.

    Give all four models the same file. Change nothing but the role and the label. No model sees another’s answer in this round. Each is told it is one seat in a multi-model test and to work as though the others were not there, which is the point: four readings that were never negotiated.

    Use a PDF if you can. Page numbers survive across interfaces better than they do in a Word file, and every criticism has to carry a quote and a page. If a model truncates the file or answers as though it read only part of it, fix that before continuing rather than working from what came back.

  3. Round 2: cross-examination

    Upload: the document and your Round 1 bundle, to the same four conversations.

    First build the bundle: one file, the four Round 1 answers in full, each under a heading naming the model and its seat. Keep them verbatim. If you tidy them here you become the hidden bottleneck, and you will tidy in the direction you already believe. If a model failed, keep its heading and say so underneath, because a silent gap reads as agreement.

    Show it

    The same shape does for the Round 2 bundle you will assemble before the arbiter. Only the contents change.

    This is the round that does the work. Round 1 hands you about twenty criticisms and most are generic. Round 2 is where they die. Every criticism carries an ID from Round 1, such as Claude-C3, so the models can name exactly which point they are attacking.

  4. Round 3, only if the gate is met

    Run this only when, for one and the same criticism, one model said keep and another said reject, both cited evidence from the document, and it is high severity. Anything less is not a fault line. Two models saying “identification is weak” and “parallel trends is not defended” are agreeing in different words. Two models reading one table and disagreeing about whether it shows what the text claims are not.

    Run it once, on one model. Whatever it leaves unsettled goes to the arbiter as an open disagreement rather than to a fourth round.

    Name the criticism before you copy. The prompt has to say which point Round 2 could not settle, so the button stays off until you give it an ID. Send it to one model, not four.

  5. The arbiter

    Upload: the document and both bundles, to a fresh ChatGPT conversation.

    Collect the four Round 2 answers into a second bundle first, in the same shape as the first one. Then open a fresh conversation, fresh even though ChatGPT was one of the four. A model that argued a position in Round 2 will defend its own phrasing when you ask it to judge. The prompt makes it merge the duplicate criticisms first and then rule on the evidence, and it forbids counting how many models agreed, because four models trained on overlapping data agreeing is weak evidence.

    Read the rejected points, the open disagreements and the minority report before the action list. Those are where a four-model run earns its cost: they exist only because four readings disagreed, so one model asked the same question has nothing to put in them.

  6. Check it yourself

    The memo is evidence, not a verdict, and this step is what makes “human in the loop” mean anything. Do it before you change a line.

    1. Search your own file for every quotation behind a surviving criticism. Drop any you cannot find. Models fabricate quotations, and a fabricated one reads exactly like a real one.
    2. Open every external source the memo names. Those points are unverified by construction. A confidently cited paper that does not exist, or exists and says something else, is the usual way a literature criticism goes wrong.
    3. Redo any arithmetic the memo leans on. Do not accept a recomputed number from any of the five conversations.
    4. Decide, and write down what you decided. You keep authorship and responsibility for the document. Where the memo and a human reader you trust disagree on a high-severity finding, default to the human and go and settle it on the evidence rather than leaving it to whoever sounded surer. Keeping a note of what you accepted and rejected is what stops a second run relitigating the first.

Lighter, and heavier

MAD sits in the middle of a range. Below it is a two-model version you can run in an afternoon. Above it are two tools that automate the same discipline for people who already work in an agent CLI.

Duel v1.7: two models instead of four

The original protocol · ChatGPT against Gemini · one long prompt, no bundles

ChatGPT forms its own view first and calls that its anchor, then argues with Gemini until they either converge or map their disagreement completely, and finally audits itself against the anchor it wrote before the argument started. You are the bridge between the two chats whenever the browsing agent cannot log in for you, which is often.

Use it when the stakes are moderate, when you are testing an idea rather than auditing a finished paper, or when four conversations is more than the question deserves.

Bob Reed at the University of Canterbury ran the same example independently and reached the same substantive conclusions in different words. Two runs agreeing is not proof that either is right, but the protocol is built to make surface variation cheap and substantive variation informative, and that is what happened.

Automated alternatives

For people already working in Claude Code or the Codex CLI · separate repositories

mad-research runs this audit as a command instead of five browser tabs: three independent role streams, an anonymised cross-critique with a prompt-injection guard on the bundle, and a fresh-context synthesis against a locked rubric, with the whole audit trail written to disk. It needs Claude Code and an authenticated Codex CLI, and it trades the four-model coverage for two providers.

paper-workshop goes past a critique memo. It builds a referee fleet for your specific paper, makes rival traditions argue each contested claim, and then, if you ask, produces a tracked-changes redline and a clean revision with your own analysis re-run. It is Claude Code only, and it is the newest and least validated of the family: the rebuild has been demonstrated end to end once.

Where these runs go wrong

  • Premature consensus. Three models make the same point and it starts to sound proven. Merge the duplicates by ID and then judge the argument once, on the evidence in your document rather than on the count.
  • Role drift. By Round 2 a model has quietly become a generic reviewer again. Restate the role in the chat and make the prior concrete.
  • Your own editing. An easy way to spoil a run is to tidy the bundles between rounds. Paste them verbatim and judge at the end, where your judgement is visible as yours.
  • A quote that is not in your paper. The last step exists for this. Do not skip it because the memo reads well.
